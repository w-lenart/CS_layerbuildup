What the model does

  1. The feeder delivers mass at a rate proportional to disk speed, set by the feed-per-rpm calibration. On top of the mean it adds a periodic pulsation at the rotation rate times the number of feed pulses per turn, plus slowly wandering random noise with a half-second correlation time.
  2. Each instant of spraying lays down a circular Gaussian footprint centred under the nozzle. The model tracks the track centreline, so the local growth rate is q·DE / (ρ·2πσ²) · exp(−x²/2σ²). Summed over one pass at constant speed this gives the standard single-track peak height h = q·DE / (ρ·v·σ·√2π), which is the height-per-pass readout.
  3. Footprint width grows linearly with stand-off from a starting spot size at the exit, σ = σ₀ + SOD·tan θ.
  4. Deposition efficiency falls off on both sides of an optimum stand-off: a bow-shock term 1 − exp(−SOD/L_b) penalises short distances, and a free-jet term penalises distances beyond the onset you set.
  5. The nozzle reverses at both ends of its path and holds stand-off relative to the mean surface, as a build with a z-step each layer would. Statistics are taken over the flat middle of the track, three footprint widths clear of the reversal points where the deposit tapers.

Why the three controls interact

A moving Gaussian footprint is a spatial low-pass filter. Feeder pulses at frequency f are laid down with wavelength λ = v/f along the track, and the footprint passes a fraction exp(−2π²σ²/λ²) of their amplitude into the layer. Raising rpm raises f and shortens the wavelength. Slowing the traverse shortens it too. Increasing stand-off widens the footprint. All three push toward smoother layers, but stand-off pays for it in deposition efficiency and peak height, and slow traverse pays for it in heat input, which this model does not track.

There is a second, sharper interaction. With a back-and-forth raster, successive passes start at different points in the pulsation cycle, so their ripples partially cancel and the layer evens out with every pass. That cancellation fails when a round trip of the nozzle spans a whole number of pulsation periods, which happens whenever one pass spans a whole or half-whole number of them. The ripple then prints in the same place every layer. In this model at 10 rpm, twenty passes at 150 or 170 mm/s averaged the variation down from about 6% to under 0.5%, but at 160 mm/s, where each pass spans exactly four periods, it stayed near 3.4% and in some starts above 6%. The window narrows as you add passes: once the timing drifts by one full cycle over the run, cancellation is essentially complete. Real feeders jitter, which softens the effect, but it costs nothing to avoid, and the note under the animation flags it when your settings land on it.

Checks you can run

  • Halve the traverse speed and the height per pass doubles. Double the feeder speed and it doubles again. That is mass conservation, and the simulated build height should track it.
  • At the default 10 rpm with random feed noise at zero and twenty passes, run 150 mm/s and then 160 mm/s. The second should finish several times less even than the first, and the note under the animation should flag it.
  • Set random feed noise to zero and stop after one pass. The height variation should come out close to the pulsation amplitude times the transmitted fraction quoted under the animation, divided by √2, since that is the standard deviation of a sine wave.

Limits you should state before anyone finds them

  • Two-dimensional centreline model of a single track. It does not model step-over between adjacent tracks, which is how area coatings are built and which introduces its own ripple.
  • Deposition efficiency does not depend on local surface slope. In reality the flanks of a tall track meet the jet at an angle and deposit less efficiently, which is why tall single-track builds narrow toward the top.
  • The deposition efficiency curve against stand-off is a phenomenological shape with adjustable length scales, not a gas-dynamics prediction. Fit it to your own efficiency-against-stand-off data, or use the deposition window tool to set the peak value.
  • Feeder pulsation is idealised as a single sine wave plus noise. A continuous-groove disk has no discrete pockets, so treat pulses per turn as an equivalent count fitted to a powder flow trace.
  • No thermal model. Slow traverse concentrates heat in the substrate, which can change efficiency and residual stress.
